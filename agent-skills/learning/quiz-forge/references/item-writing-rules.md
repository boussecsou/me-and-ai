# Item-Writing Rules

Based on item-writing guidelines validated by research in educational measurement (Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez, 2002; Burton et al., 1991; Brame, 2013, Vanderbilt CFT) and on Rodriguez's (2005) meta-analysis on the optimal number of options. Used by `SKILL.md` at step 3.

Write the actual item content in the target language decided in the Language section of `SKILL.md`. The rules below are language-agnostic; the examples are given in English but the same logic applies in any language.

## Anatomy of an item

An MCQ item has two parts: the stem and the options, one of which is the correct answer (the key) while the rest are distractors.

## Stem rules

- **Self-contained.** The learner must be able to answer just by reading the stem, before even seeing the options. If the stem only makes sense after reading the choices, it's a bad item.
  - Bad: "Which of the following is true about photosynthesis?"
  - Good: "Which gas does photosynthesis primarily consume?"
- **One idea tested.** Never mix two concepts in the same item.
- **No irrelevant information.** Anything not needed to answer just adds reading load without value (exception: scenario-based items for Apply/Analyze levels, where the context is part of what's being tested).
- **Active voice, direct phrasing, vocabulary matched to the audience's level.**
- **Avoid negatives.** "Which of the following is NOT..." is a source of reading errors unrelated to actual mastery of the subject. If negation is pedagogically necessary (e.g., spotting an exception), make it unmistakably visible (caps, bold) and never stack a negative stem on top of negatively-phrased options.
- **Independent from item to item.** The answer to one question must never depend on having correctly answered a previous one.

## Option rules

- **3 to 4 options, no more.** Rodriguez's (2005) meta-analysis of 80 years of research shows 3 well-constructed options measure as well as a longer list: beyond that, extra distractors are almost never chosen (they don't "function") and just lengthen reading time.
- **Homogeneous** in content, length, and grammatical register. A correct answer that's noticeably longer, more precise, or more technically phrased than the distractors is the most classic and most exploited guessing cue.
- **Mutually exclusive**, never overlapping between two options.
- **Plausible and homogeneous.** A distractor no serious learner would ever pick tests nothing; it just artificially inflates the success rate. The best distractor corresponds to a genuinely documented or predictable error on this exact topic (confusion between two similar concepts, a typical calculation mistake, over-generalizing a rule).
- **Logical order when one exists** (chronological, numerical, alphabetical), otherwise randomized, never an order that systematically places the correct answer in the same spot.
- **Explicitly forbidden:**
  - "All of the above" / "None of the above" (they turn the question into a test of elimination logic rather than knowledge, and are statistically exploitable cues).
  - Obviously absurd or off-topic distractors.
  - Grammatical cues (an article or agreement in the stem that only fits one option).
  - Overlapping numeric ranges between options (e.g., "10 to 20," "15 to 25" that overlap).

## Common flaws generated by an unconstrained LLM (check for these explicitly)

These flaws recur in studies evaluating the quality of LLM-generated items (see e.g. evaluations of GPT-3.5/GPT-4 item quality, or the item-writing-flaw (IWF) taxonomies used as audit rubrics):

1. "Superficial" distractors: obvious rephrasings or trivial variations that fool no one.
2. Confusing "correct" with "best-worded": the model sometimes generates a correct answer alongside an option that's almost equally defensible, creating unintended ambiguity.
3. Hallucination: a fact in the stem, key, or a distractor that isn't actually supported by the source (see step 4 of `SKILL.md`).
4. Systematic under-calibration: a tendency to write pure-recall questions even when a "hard" quiz was requested, because that's the easiest format to generate.
5. Length/style cues: the correct answer mechanically standing out from the distractors without that being intended.

## Good vs. bad example

**Bad item:**

> Which of the following relates to the HTTP protocol?
> A. It uses port 80 by default, unless configured otherwise at the server or proxy level
> B. Cats
> C. None of the above
> D. All of the above

Flaws: stem is meaningless without the options, distractor B is absurd, C and D are forbidden, option A is oddly long and technical compared to the others.

**Good item (Apply level):**

> A web server normally responds on port 80. A client can't connect even though the server is running and no firewall is active. What's the most likely cause?
> A. The server is listening on a port other than 80
> B. The client is using HTTPS instead of HTTP
> C. The domain name isn't pointing to the correct IP address
>
> (Three plausible, distinct causes of the same symptom, options homogeneous in length, a real scenario to diagnose rather than a fact to recall.)
